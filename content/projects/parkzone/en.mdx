## The problem

In Mexico City, parking meters are run by **different apps depending on the borough**. A driver who moves around the city has to install and learn several apps, each with its own sign-up, interface and payment method.

I analysed the three apps that dominate the market — Blinkay, Mueve Ciudad and Parkum — and their reviews repeat the same complaints over and over:

| App | Recurring complaints in reviews |
|---|---|
| **Blinkay** | Schedule sync errors · balance top-ups · GPS malfunctions |
| **Mueve Ciudad** | Loading and sync failures · no GPS coverage · complicated interface |
| **Parkum** | Access and operation issues · poor customer service · vehicle-lock failures |

**Why it matters:** this fragmentation isn't just an annoyance. It turns into **avoidable fines** when a driver can't pay in time because the app for that zone fails — or simply isn't installed.

## Information architecture

Before drawing a single screen, I validated how users expect functionality to be grouped — not how I assumed it should be. I ran a **card sort with 25 people in UXtweak** on the candidate functions (geolocation, payment, time selection, vehicle management, account, notifications) and analysed the results with a similarity matrix, a dendrogram (Actual Agreement Method) and a standardization grid.

**What I found:**

- The parking-flow functions (time, rates, payment, extension) are perceived as **one coherent group**: they should live together, not be scattered across menus.
- Administrative functions (account, payment methods, vehicle) form **separate sections**, lower priority in daily use.
- Notifications and log-out are perceived as **peripheral**: they shouldn't compete for space with frequent actions.

**So I decided** to restructure the original model — grouped by screen type — into a model grouped **by user intent**: Home & Navigation · Parking & Payment · Vehicle Management · Account & Settings · Notifications & Support.

## Validation

Before calling the design done, I put it through two kinds of validation.

### Heuristic evaluation (Nielsen)

I reviewed the prototype against the 10 usability principles. It did well on consistency, match with the real world and user freedom, but I found **two real gaps**:

| Gap | What was missing | How it was addressed |
|---|---|---|
| **Error prevention** | Nothing stopped you from trying to pay without selecting a time. | In the coded version, the confirm button is **disabled** until a valid time is set. |
| **System error handling** | What happens if a payment fails wasn't defined. | Documented as a next step: payment error states (declined, offline) were left out of the MVP scope. |

### Usability testing with 5 users

I designed a real scenario: park at meter A248, pay for 1 hour, check the remaining time, extend by 30 minutes and review the payment history.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| U1 | Yes | 120 s | 2 | “I couldn't easily find the button to extend the time.” |
| U2 | Yes | 95 s | 0 | “Everything was very intuitive, but I'd add a more visible icon for the history.” |
| U3 | No (task 4) | 180 s | 3 | “I didn't understand how to confirm the payment.” |
| U4 | Yes | 110 s | 1 | “The design is clear, but some text is small.” |
| U5 | Yes | 130 s | 2 | “Easy to use, but it took me a while to find the option to monitor the status.” |

**The most repeated issue:** low visibility of the “Extend time” button and the history icon. So I decided to increase the size and contrast of those specific elements in the next iteration — not redesign the whole flow — because the rest of the metrics confirmed the structure already worked.

## From prototype to code

The Figma prototype solved the problem on paper, but I wanted to prove the solution also held up in a real product: with state, real-time calculations and data that persists between screens.

I rebuilt the full prototype as an **interactive Next.js app**, presented in an iPhone frame like the original prototype. The parking session runs a **live countdown**, the total is calculated in real time from the rate ($3.50 / 15 min), and the heuristic-evaluation findings were addressed directly in the code.

## Reflection and learnings

**1 · Fewer users, less certainty.** With only 5 users in testing, the results are exploratory, not conclusive. If I repeated the process, I'd run a second round after applying the fixes instead of assuming a visual tweak is enough. In the card sort I'd also test the category names, not just the grouping.

**2 · The problem wasn't size, it was state.** The user who failed the confirm-payment task didn't say he couldn't see the button — he said he didn't understand how to confirm. Nothing told him his selection was already valid. That's why, in the coded version, the button stays disabled until a valid time is selected, instead of just being made bigger.

**3 · The prototype can “lie”.** In Figma, two layers can look aligned even when positioned by eye. When I coded the meter tooltip with real positions on the map, an offset appeared that the static design never revealed. Same with the hamburger menu: in Figma it was a button with no action; coding it forced me to decide what actually happens when it opens.

**4 · Miguel Hidalgo isn't all of Mexico City.** The meter selector is built for 2 or 3 visible pins at a time. At city scale, with thousands of meters, it would need map clustering and a search by street or neighbourhood. I also assumed a fixed rate; each borough has its own, so that value would have to come from the backend per zone.
