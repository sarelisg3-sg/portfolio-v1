<Callout title="Independent research">
No access to Moises' internal data. The business challenge was built from desk research on a public freemium model. All participants were anonymized.
</Callout>

## Context and problem

<Quote who="Starting point of the project">
I asked 6 musicians why they weren't paying for Moises. None of them said it was expensive.
</Quote>

**Moises** is a freemium app for musicians: it separates the stems of a song with AI (vocals, drums, bass, other), detects chords and changes key and tempo. As in any freemium model, the business doesn't live off the people who download the app — it lives off the percentage who convert to paid. Moving that conversion from 3% to 4% isn't a 1% improvement: it's **33% more revenue** on the same user base, without spending a peso on acquisition.

**Research question:** what motivates or holds back a musician when it comes to paying for a tool like Moises?

### Starting hypotheses

Stated before doing any research — so I could be wrong in public, not to confirm them.

| | Hypothesis |
|---|---|
| **H1** | They don't convert because they don't know the premium features exist, not because they don't want them. |
| **H2** | The paywall appears at a moment of frustration, not of perceived value. |
| **H3** | The free tier limit is generous enough — there's no real pressure to pay. |
| **H4** | The main barrier is the price or the payment method. |

## Desk research: the first signal

A month of desk research — over 46 reviews read on the App Store, Play Store and PissedConsumer, plus 28 TikTok screenshots — already pointed to something none of the hypotheses fully captured: the most repeated pattern wasn't "it's expensive". It was **distrust of recurring billing** and of being able to cancel or change plans without friction.

## Strategy: mixed methods

**Objective:** understand the motivators and barriers that determine whether a musician pays for premium features on Moises, to produce product recommendations that improve free→premium conversion.

Qualitative tells you the *why*; quantitative tells you the *how much*. And without a usability test that sets a baseline on the current product, there's no way to prove afterwards that the research improved anything.

| Method | What it contributes |
|---|---|
| **6 in-depth interviews** | The why — including behaviours people don't declare in closed forms. |
| **Survey (NPS + SUS + Likert)** | The how much — sizes at scale the patterns seen in interviews. |
| **Usability test (5 people)** | A Task Success Rate baseline on the real flow — evidence of impact, not just opinion. |

**Deliberate recruiting.** The 6 interviews purposely covered different relationships with payment: shares an account, pays individually, uses only the free tier, downgraded. The 6-question screener avoided direct yes/no questions about the product, so as not to give away the focus of the study from the start.

## Qualitative: from 6 interviews to 16 insights

The interviews produced **16 insights**, tagged by type (pain point, motivation, usage or payment barrier, unexpected behaviour, avoidance strategy). They were grouped by hand in Miro into **8 clusters**.

The original screener filtered by "most used app" and was discarding 93% of the real sample due to a design error. It was fixed mid-campaign and documented as a pivot, not hidden.

A SUS of 76.1 — above the benchmark, and higher still among individual payers (**86.6**) — confirms what the interviews were already saying: **Moises doesn't have a core usability problem.** The conversion barrier is something else.

## The pivot: when two data points contradict each other

An interview insight seemed to contradict a review collected months earlier during desk research:

<Quote who="Interview 5">
It's nice that it's already streamlined and has a cancel subscription button… having that freedom is great.
</Quote>

<Quote who="App Store review">
I have the Pro plan… and I tried to change it to Premium… all I get is “add payment method”.
</Quote>

Instead of averaging or discarding one of the two data points, the contradiction was treated as a hypothesis to verify: **cancelling entirely and downgrading could be two different flows**, with different experiences. It was added as an explicit task to the moderated usability test.

### Result: 5 people, 2 tasks, on the real app

| Participant | Task A — activate premium value | Task B — downgrade without cancelling |
|---|---|---|
| P1 (Pro plan) | ✅ | ❌ Reaches the real Stripe portal; only sees “Cancel subscription” |
| P2 (free plan) | ✅ | ⚠️ Inconclusive — only demonstrates the free→Premium upgrade |
| P3 (Premium plan) | ⚠️ | ❌ Reaches “Manage subscription” but doesn't enter the flow |
| P4 (Premium plan) | ⚠️ | ❌ Same drop-off point |
| P5 (Pro plan) | ❌ | ❌ Dead end, no visible control at all |
| **Task Success Rate** | **40% (2/5)** | **0% (0/5)** |

The most decisive case: participant 1, the only session with a real Pro account, followed the correct path (Plans → Manage subscription) and the app redirected her to Stripe's real billing portal — verified with a screenshot, not just her report.

**Conclusion:** there was no contradiction — they're two different flows with different experiences. Cancelling entirely is easy; switching to a cheaper plan without cancelling is not. The study doesn't prove that downgrading is technically impossible in the backend: it proves that, following the available navigation paths, no participant could discover or complete it.

## Synthesis: impact–effort matrix

The opportunities came directly from the 16 insights and the affinity map, prioritized by impact and effort.

| Opportunity | Impact | Effort | Quadrant |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contextual premium-value banner | High | Low | **Quick win** |
| Onboarding segmented by role | Medium | Low | **Quick win** |
| “Manage my plan” with a visible downgrade | Medium | Low | **Quick win** |
| Changelog / roadmap visible in the account | Medium | Low | **Quick win** |
| Formal duo/family plan | High | Medium | Big bet |
| Perpetual / lifetime plan | High | High | Big bet |
| Messaging for the segment with ethical objections to AI | Low | — | Reconsider |

### The insight that didn't fit any hypothesis

<Quote who="Interview 2">
I'm strongly against using artificial intelligence to create music… for my own ethical reasons, I wouldn't use Moises in that context.
</Quote>

It surfaced with only 2 of 6 interviews completed. It's not a price barrier or a trust barrier: it's a values stance. The right recommendation isn't to redesign anything for this segment — it's to **stop spending conversion effort there**, because it probably never converts no matter how much the product improves.

## From research to prototype

From this evidence, a Figma prototype was built: **4 key screens**, not a full redesign, on the exact points where the research found friction, connected with real clicks.

**How the evidence changed the proposed architecture:**

- A **“Manage my plan”** section with the downgrade at the same level of visibility as cancelling — justified directly by the 0% on task B.
- A **contextual premium-value banner**, at the exact moment the user hits the free limit, not hidden in an FAQ.
- **Onboarding with a role question** (performer vs. producer/composer) — the free tier is already “enough” for one of the two segments.

## Recommendations and next steps

**Recommendations, by urgency:**

1. Give “change plan” the same visibility “cancel” already has — the most urgent: the test confirmed it in 0 of 5 cases.
2. Show the value of premium right when the user hits the free limit.
3. Explore a perpetual plan and a formal duo plan, capturing as legitimate revenue a behaviour that already exists for free today (shared accounts, multiple accounts).
4. Stop investing conversion effort in the segment with ethical objections to AI — it doesn't convert with better design.

**Next steps:**

- Implement the “Manage my plan” screen and re-measure the Task Success Rate of task B — the goal is to move it up from 0%.
- Cross-check with Moises' internal analytics whether the invisible downgrade correlates with avoidable cancellations.
- Pilot the duo/family plan with the profile that already pays and uses the app as a couple (direct evidence in interviews).
- Redirect conversion-messaging effort towards the 4 quick wins in the matrix.

## Business impact

This research turned a business assumption (“the problem is the price”) into a specific, measured and actionable root cause: the distrust isn't about the amount, it's about the process of changing plans — confirmed with **0% success on a real task**, not with an opinion. That avoids months of misdirected effort on discounts or pricing redesigns that wouldn't have solved anything, and points design investment at exactly the four lowest-effort, highest-impact changes.
