> **Self-initiated** research project, with no commercial relationship or access to internal Moises data. The business challenge was built from desk research on a public freemium model. All participants were anonymized.

## The problem

**Moises** is a freemium app for musicians that uses AI to separate a song's stems, detect chords, and change key and tempo. As in any freemium model, the business doesn't live off who downloads the app — it lives off the percentage that converts to paid. Moving that conversion from 3% to 4% isn't a 1% improvement: it's **33% more revenue** on the same user base, without spending a peso on acquisition.

**Research question:** what motivates or holds back a musician from paying for a tool like Moises?

A month of desk research (46+ reviews from the App Store, Play Store and PissedConsumer, plus 28 TikTok screenshots) already pointed to something no initial hypothesis fully captured: the most repeated pattern wasn't "it's expensive" — it was **distrust in recurring billing** and in being able to cancel or change plans without friction.

## Mixed method

- **6 in-depth interviews**, with deliberate recruiting (shares an account, pays individually, uses the free tier only, downgraded a plan) — the *why* behind the behavior.
- **Closed survey** (NPS + SUS + Likert): 59 total responses, 22 complete — the *how much*, at scale.
- **Usability testing with 5 people**, on the real Moises app, measuring a baseline before proposing any change.

The 6 interviews produced 16 insights, grouped by hand into an affinity map with 8 clusters, and separately cross-checked against Miro AI's automatic clustering on the same notes — without telling it the groups — as a way to validate the qualitative analysis.

## The pivot: when two data points contradict each other

One interview insight seemed, on the surface, to contradict a review collected months earlier during desk research:

> "It's nice that it's already systematized and has a cancel-subscription button... having that freedom is great." — Fakir, interview

> "I have the pro plan... and tried switching it to premium... it only lets me add a payment method." — Moy HL, App Store review

Instead of averaging out or discarding one of the two data points, the contradiction was treated as a hypothesis to verify: **canceling and downgrading could be two different flows**, with different experiences. It was added as an explicit task to the usability test.

**Result — 5 people, 2 tasks, on the real app:**

| Task | Success |
|---|---|
| A — activate premium value | 40% (2/5) |
| B — downgrade without canceling | **0% (0/5)** |

The most decisive case: Mari, the only session with a real Pro account, followed the correct path (Plans → Manage subscription) and the app redirected her to Stripe's real billing portal — verified with an actual screenshot, not just her self-report. The only visible action there was "Cancel subscription": no control to change plans.

**Conclusion:** canceling entirely is indeed easy; switching to a cheaper plan without canceling is not. The study doesn't prove downgrading is technically impossible on the backend — it proves that, following the navigation paths available, no participant could discover or execute it.

## The numbers

- **Final NPS: +13.6** (average 7.27)
- **SUS: 76.1** — above the benchmark (68), and even higher among individual payers (86.6)

The high SUS confirms what the interviews already suggested: Moises doesn't have a core usability problem. The conversion barrier is something else — and the task B test pinpoints it precisely.

## From research to prototype

Based on this evidence, a Figma prototype was built: **4 key screens** (not a full redesign) covering the exact points where the research found friction, connected with real clicks — role-based onboarding, a contextual premium-value banner shown right when the user hits the free limit, a plan comparison, and a "Manage my plan" section with downgrade at the same visibility level as cancel.

**Recommendations prioritized in an impact-effort matrix:**

- Give "change plan" the same visibility "cancel" already has — the most urgent one: the test confirmed it in 0 of 5 cases.
- Show premium value right at the moment the user hits the free limit, not hidden in a FAQ.
- Explore a lifetime plan and a formal duo/family plan, capturing as legitimate revenue a behavior that already happens for free (account sharing, multiple accounts).
- Don't spend conversion effort on the segment with an ethical objection to AI — it won't convert with better design, no matter how much the product improves.
